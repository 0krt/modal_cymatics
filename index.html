About read me

What is this?
A Chladni plate is a flat sheet of metal with sand on it. Vibrate it at the right pitch and the sand stops being a mess and snaps into a sharp geometric pattern. Ernst Chladni was doing this in 1787; Napoleon watched it and put up prize money.

Why does the sand move?
A vibrating plate does not go up and down all in one piece. Parts of it swing hard while other parts barely move at all. The still lines are called nodes. Sand sitting on a violently shaking part gets thrown around until it happens to land somewhere still — and then it stays. So the sand is not drawing the vibration. It is drawing the places where there is no vibration. Fine powder does the opposite: it is light enough for the moving air to carry, so it piles up at the loudest spots instead.

What is a mode?
A plate cannot vibrate in just any shape. Like a guitar string that will only give you its fundamental and its overtones, a plate has a fixed catalogue of standing patterns it is willing to hold — its modes. Each one has its own pitch. Play that pitch and that pattern appears; play something in between and you mostly get mud. That is why sweeping a tone generator across a plate is so dramatic: nothing, nothing, nothing — then suddenly a perfect star.

What decides the pitches?
Size — bigger plates are lower, and steeply so: double the width and every pitch drops by a factor of four.
Stiffness and thickness — a thicker or stiffer plate is higher. Steel sings higher than aluminium of the same size.
Shape and how it is held — a square, a circle and a triangle have completely different catalogues. Clamping the rim rather than letting it flap raises every pitch and changes every pattern.

What makes the patterns intricate?
Sometimes two entirely different modes happen to share the same pitch — degenerate modes. They both wake up at once and add together, and where you touch the plate to drive it decides how they blend, so one frequency can give a whole family of figures. A driver sitting exactly on a mode's node cannot wake it at all — which is why moving the drive point makes half the catalogue vanish.

a real plate is fourth-order (biharmonic), so doubling the frequency scales the wavenumber by √2, not 2. the membrane setting is the ordinary wave equation for comparison.

free — rim moves freely, the classic loose Chladni plate.
supported — rim pinned to zero but free to tilt.
clamped — rim pinned and held flat (w = w′ = 0). the stiffest case, so every mode sits higher, and a boundary layer forms at the rim.
mixed c–f — clamped along x, free along y: a rectangular plate held on two opposite edges only.

the rectangle uses beam eigenfunctions per axis (the Ritz construction); the disc uses Bessel modes, with the clamped case needing the modified-Bessel companion. the ring is supported on both rims, and the equilateral triangle uses the exact Lamé modes built by reflecting six plane waves — both are only defined for the supported case here.

real units converts the abstract frequency scale into hertz for an actual sheet: f = π F h cp / (2 L² √12), with cp = √(E / ρ(1−ν²)). the fourth-power in L is the whole story of plate tuning — double the size and every pitch drops two octaves. thickness scales it linearly the other way.

Drive

the plate is a linear filter: waveform only matters as harmonic content. each partial excites whatever modes it lands near. relative partial phase cannot move the sand — only the amplitude spectrum can.

partial focus is a bandpass across the harmonic series. at off the whole spectrum drives the plate; sweep it up and it isolates one partial at a time, so you can hear and see a single harmonic pull its own set of modes. focus width is how many neighbouring partials come with it — narrow it to about 0.2 for a clean solo.

controls grey out when the waveform does not use them: a sine already has one partial.

snap to resonance quantises the drive to the nearest eigenfrequency of the current plate, so a sweep lands on figures instead of passing between them. the slider still moves freely — only the value it reports is snapped. the scroll wheel then steps mode by mode.

Excitation point

a driver sitting on a mode's nodal line cannot excite it at all. drag on the plate to move the driver and watch half the catalogue vanish. corner drive couples to everything.

shift-click the plate to pin a damping finger (up to four; shift-click one again to lift it). a finger only damps the modes that actually move underneath it — park one on a nodal line and that mode rings on untouched, which is exactly how you pick a single figure out of a degenerate pair by hand.

Grains

coarse sand collects at nodes. very fine powder does the opposite — acoustic streaming in the air carries it to the antinodes. both at once runs two populations in two colours, so the nodal and antinodal figures appear together; fine fraction is the split.

feed rate continuously recycles grains back onto the plate, oldest first, as if you were sprinkling steadily while it runs. at zero the population is static.

hop threshold is the local amplitude a grain needs before it will move at all — real sand only migrates once the plate's acceleration beats gravity. jitter is random bouncing, scaled by the local amplitude, so it competes with the crawl and fuzzes the lines.

Audio

synthesises the actual drive signal — the same partial amplitudes and phases that are feeding the plate, as a periodic wave. changing waveform, partial count, tilt or pulse width changes what you hear and what the plate does, together.

pitch per unit sets the audible hertz for one unit of modal frequency, since the frequency slider is in plate units, not hertz. at 8 Hz/unit a drive of f 25 sounds at 200 Hz.

real hz ignores that slider and plays the frequency the plate would actually sound at, from the size, thickness and material set under Plate — you hear the sheet you drew.

Modes

the modes the current drive is actually exciting, strongest first.

rectangle — (m,n): the number of half-wave divisions along each axis. (3,2) has three across and two down. natural frequency goes as m² + n² for a square plate; stretching the aspect ratio or clamping the edges pushes it up.

equilateral triangle — (p,q): the two lattice indices of the Lamé solution. frequency goes as p² + pq + q². the ′ twin is the mirror-antisymmetric partner — it exists only when p ≠ q, which is exactly why the diagonal modes are single and every other level comes in a degenerate pair.

disc / ring — (n,s): n nodal diameters (lines through the centre), s nodal circles (rings). a trailing ′ marks the rotated twin of the same mode — it has the same frequency but sits 90°/n around, and the drive point decides how the two mix.

f is the mode's natural frequency in the same units as the frequency slider — park the slider on one of these numbers and that mode rings on its own. equal f values are degenerate: they resonate together and superpose, which is where the intricate figures come from. the bar is how hard the drive is currently pushing each one.

Presets

LFO

two free-running modulators. each has a list of targets, and each target its own bipolar amount — the fraction of that parameter's full range the lfo sweeps. modulation is added on top of the slider rather than replacing it, so the slider stays yours: move it and the whole modulated swing moves with it. the readout turns blue while a value is being driven.

plate shape, edge condition and dispersion are steppable targets — the lfo quantises to a whole setting and only fires on a change, so a slow square wave flips between two plates instead of thrashing the solver.

s&h holds a fresh random value for a whole cycle, so rate is the step rate. phase offsets one lfo against the other. rate and depth are themselves midi targets. settings persist locally.
